# Chapter 10 · Deployment / runtime

Part of the AIW handbook gap review (AIW-HANDBOOK-GAP-REVIEW-2026-10-30.md). Gaps verified against the AIW source (paths relative to AIW-V5-Local-Source) and challenged by a skeptic; handbook references are "Handbook › heading (section id)".


**How AIW compares with the handbooks.** The delivery, infra, appdesign/intdata and method handbooks treat deployment and runtime architecture as a chain of decisions that must each be qualified. The architect declares the failure set and the minimum service that must survive it, chooses location and topology through gates, and sizes from a workload model and shared-dependency envelopes. Recovery is designed as a timed, gated procedure that ends at a business endpoint. Release and rollback mechanisms are chosen by their prerequisites and reversal scope, and operation is accepted only on evidence of matching scope. AIW's Chapter 10 describes one deployment well as structure: environments, zones with free-text failure domains, one runtime plan per environment and asset, placements and contract-bound paths, checked by about 30 deterministic rules plus zone and failure-domain walkthroughs. Its doctrine holds throughout: recovery figures are labelled assumed, walkthroughs are marked simulated, design review is kept apart from exercise evidence, evidence goes stale on change, selection and acceptance are person-only, and Sol only drafts. But the decisive content is mostly prose checked only for presence (fencing, backupPlan, readiness, rollout, rollback, recoveryPlan, monitoring, runbook). Its numbers are single assumed values per plan. They are never added up across plans, never tied to a replication or acknowledgement mechanism, and never compared with the recorded per-zone placements. Much of the handbooks' arithmetic does exist (zone-loss sizing, connections against maxConnections, single-unit ceilings, backlog drain, restore-time budget, nested retry exposure), but it lives in Chapter 3 design tasks, the Chapter 8 execution reading and the Chapter 11 desk, runs on global or hand-typed inputs, and is not bound to Chapter 10 plans or targets. So the gaps are mostly about binding and typed records, not missing calculators: failure set and degraded modes, common-cause and survivor readings, resource envelopes, recovery objectives and stage graphs, recovery sets, write authority, reopening gates, evidence of matching scope, and typed release, rollback and exposure records. One concrete defect was found independently by three shards: the desk and ARS availability reading counts zone records, not independent failure domains, so it can read 'holds' while Chapter 10 itself warns of a single domain. Every fix is a new record, a deterministic reading or a Validate check; none changes the authority chain.

### C10-01 · No declared failure set, continuity claim or degraded-mode contract drives the deployment design  
*Severity high · effort L*

- **Handbooks:** Each resilience and topology claim names the exact failure cause it survives (replica, zone, region, regional dependency, defective configuration, shared authority service). It also states the minimum business service during that failure, the qualified survivor capacity and what it does not cover. Degraded modes are defined per business function (what continues, what is deferred, what is rejected, admission before durable acceptance, traffic priority, customer wording) and the service owner approves them before any crisis. The topology is chosen from that failure cause, preferring the simplest arrangement that meets the obligation. A multi-zone arrangement must never be presented as regional disaster recovery. / Deriving a deployment architecture starts from the failure and trust boundaries the business must survive. The design states the correlated-failure tolerance (allowed domain losses), the topology key, the maximum replica skew and a survivor-capacity target, and labels must reflect governed infrastructure facts. Recovery is designed against declared scenarios that say what is lost and what survives, including a separate scenario for losing recovery resources too, with an RTO/RPO matrix per scenario. HA scheduling settings are intent, not proof of independent domains. Placement exceptions are visible, scoped and time-bounded. / Specify reduced service explicitly as an outcome contract approved by the journey owner. It states which capabilities remain, the allowed data age, the reduced capacity envelope (for example 120 of 200 Mbit/s for critical traffic), priority and shed order, paused workloads, user messaging, and entry and exit criteria. Degradation must be monotone in authority: it never broadens permission or invents completion. A fallback counts as continuity only if its qualified capacity clears demand within the recovery horizon. Otherwise, define admission, deferral and communication, and have the owner accept a bounded degraded service. Reopening budgets backlog, probes and new traffic.

- **Why it matters:** Without a declared fault model the topology can be neither justified nor falsified. The walkthrough and the desk answer 'does something keep running', not 'does the agreed minimum service survive the nominated failure'. Degraded behaviour then gets invented during the incident instead of being accepted by the service owner in advance. The handbooks treat that as the core of a defensible deployment design.
- **Recommendation:** Add a continuity-claim / runtime.scenario record (FS-nnn) per environment, and per business service (or group of plans) once that record exists, for each nominated failure cause: instance or replica, zone, failure domain, region, regional or provider dependency, defective release, control plane, or a correlated set naming zones or domains. ### C10-31 · Startup, readiness, liveness and business admission are one prose field, with no drain, stop or termination contract  
*Severity medium · effort M*

- **Handbooks:** Startup, readiness (should receive new work), liveness (restart would help) and business admission (safe to create an obligation) are distinct signals. Readiness should not depend on every downstream service: a rail outage must not remove all API targets when bounded admission is safe. Route-specific readiness keeps status and reconciliation available.
